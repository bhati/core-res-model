Domain Expertise

(Design-Level Specification — Horizontal)

⸻

1. What Is Expertise

Expertise is what makes a domain useful to a person. Without it, the domain is structure without substance — entities without understanding, tools without judgment, states without navigation.

This definition is derived from first principles: what does any real-world expert — doctor, lawyer, financial advisor, nutritionist, coach — actually bring?

⸻

2. Components of Expertise

2.1 Domain Knowledge

The expert knows the field — not just facts but relationships, causality, and nuance. They know what things are, why they matter, how they interact, and what happens when you change one thing.

This is not artifact knowledge (how to build a plan) — it is entity and concept knowledge (what food does in a body, what a meal is, how cooking changes nutrition).

2.2 Assessment

The expert can evaluate a person's situation. They ask the right questions. They notice what matters. They synthesize scattered signals into a coherent picture.

Assessment is not data collection — it is meaning-making. Two users with identical attributes may need completely different approaches.

2.3 Recommendation

The expert can suggest what to do — not any suggestion, but the RIGHT suggestion for THIS person in THIS situation. Recommendations are contextual, proportional, and actionable.

A bad expert recommends the textbook answer. A good expert recommends what will actually work for the person in front of them.

2.4 Communication

The expert can explain their reasoning, frame things appropriately, and adapt their language to the person. They can simplify without dumbing down. They can be precise without overwhelming.

Communication is shaped by the person — their knowledge level, emotional state, engagement level, cultural context.

2.5 Empathy

The expert understands the person's emotional state, motivations, fears, and constraints. They care about the person, not just the problem.

Empathy is not softness — it is accurate perception. An empathetic expert knows when to push and when to ease off, when to congratulate and when to be honest.

2.6 Evidence-Based Reasoning

The expert draws from established knowledge, research, and best practices. They don't operate on intuition alone. When evidence conflicts with intuition, they examine both.

Best practices exist per domain. They are default recommendations that work for most people most of the time — and the expert knows when to deviate.

2.7 Boundaries

The expert knows what they do NOT know. They know when to defer, when to refer, and what is outside their scope.

Boundaries are non-negotiable. A nutrition expert does not diagnose. A fitness expert does not prescribe medication. Boundaries protect the person.

2.8 Adaptation

The expert adjusts over time. Initial recommendations may change as they learn more about the person, as circumstances shift, or as the person's goals evolve.

Adaptation is not inconsistency — it is responsiveness. An expert who never changes their recommendation despite new evidence is not adapting. An expert who changes every session is not grounding.

⸻

3. How Expertise Activates

Expertise is not always fully active. Different moments call for different components:

First encounter → Assessment + Communication + Empathy dominate.
Goal-setting → Recommendation + Evidence-Based Reasoning dominate.
Daily use → Domain Knowledge + Communication dominate.
Periodic review → Assessment + Adaptation dominate.
Difficulty or failure → Empathy + Boundaries + Adaptation dominate.

The mix shifts based on the moment, the person, and the situation. No single component is always primary.

⸻

4. Expertise Per Domain

Each domain instantiates expertise by defining:
	•	What domain knowledge is required (entities, concepts, relationships)
	•	How assessment works in this domain (what questions to ask, what to observe)
	•	What recommendation looks like (what can be suggested, at what granularity)
	•	How communication should be shaped (domain-specific language, cultural sensitivity)
	•	What empathy reads (domain-specific emotional patterns and fears)
	•	What evidence base exists (research, guidelines, best practices)
	•	Where the boundaries are (what the domain does not do)
	•	How adaptation works (what signals trigger change, how fast to adjust)

⸻

5. Expertise Is Not

	•	Expertise is not artifact construction — how to build a meal plan is a tool concern.
	•	Expertise is not orchestration — when to invoke which tool is a system concern.
	•	Expertise is not policy — what is not allowed is a constraint concern.
	•	Expertise is not data — what is true about the user is a context concern.

Expertise is the judgment layer that reads context, respects policy, and decides what the domain should do for this person right now.

⸻

6. One-Line Definition

Domain Expertise is the combination of knowledge, assessment, recommendation, communication, empathy, evidence-based reasoning, boundaries, and adaptation that makes a domain useful to a person — instantiated per domain, activated per moment.
